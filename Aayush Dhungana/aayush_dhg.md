# The Quest for "True" "Freedom": A Treatise on Free Will vs. Determinism

In our Present World, the relationship between free will and determinism is often framed as a quiet, ongoing war. 

On one side stands a deterministic universe, where every thought, impulse, and choice is the necessary downstream consequence of prior physical causes — rendering human agency a comforting fiction. 

On the other side stands the ideal of "true" "freedom," demanding an uncaused, independent self capable of stepping outside the laws of nature to originate action from absolute nothingness.

This binary creates a profound intellectual and existential exhaustion to the subjective mind: if strict determinism is accepted, the lived experience of deliberation feels like a cruel joke, yet if absolute independence from causality is demanded, the pursuit goes on to lead towards a phantom that cannot exist in any coherent physical or logical reality.  

This essay thus attempts to offer a different posture in this age-old debate. It does not ask the reader to accept a new dogma or suspend scientific reasoning. Instead, it proposes a conceptual framework for the reader to choose to adopt - a potential reconciliation of our embedded place within this universe which has granted (to) us the 'genuine reality' of participation.


## 1. The Trap of the Isolated Self

It is important to state right from the outset that the quest for "true" "freedom" fails precisely because of where it begins: with the assumption of an isolated agent. 

Typically, a sharp line is drawn around the individual, imagining the self on the inside and the vast causal universe on the outside, the vast casual universe continuously intruding upon the autonomy of the self.

To appreciate a different view, we can turn towards eastern philosophy. In Eastern philosophical traditions, the concept of Māyā examines this apparent separation between the individual self (the aatman) and the vast collective outwards - universe (the brahman). Māyā does not imply that the physical world is a literal illusion (as commonly misunderstood), but rather that treating apparent practical boundaries as absolute ontologies is a mistake made by the subjective mind. This concept is reinforced by Pratītyasamutpāda (dependent origination), which directs attention away from self-sufficient existence and toward the conditions through which phenomena arise.

In all this, physics offers a striking analogy here through the phenomena of quantum decoherence. A system does not become observable and stable by being perfectly isolated; rather, it derives its classical appearance precisely through continuous interaction and entanglement with its environment. An individual is not a solitary entity trapped inside a universe, but a relational process continuous with it. Same as how origin does not erase contribution, being produced by the universe does not go on to prevent a system from being causally productive within it.

## 2. Constraint as Reality's "Anti-Cheat" System

Constraint is routinely mistaken for imprisonment. 

Yet structure should not be perceived as the enemy of agency; it is instead the very space in which agency becomes intelligible.

This relationship can be illuminated by the concept of Līlā—the view of existence as play. 

A game without rules is not an arena of supreme liberty; it is chaotic noise. To have a meaningful game, there must be strict rules that preserve consequence. 

In theoretical physics, the ****chronology protection conjecture**** (proposed by Dr. Stephen Hawking in 1992) suggests that the universe actively prevents closed timelike curves (backward time travel). Conceptually, this acts as reality's "anti-cheat" system. If unrestricted backward causal influence were possible, actions could retroactively rewrite their own conditions, and thus, consequence, memory, and learning would become impossible.

The conceptual vocabulary of Karma also points to this exact causal continuity: constraints ensure that conditions are inherited from past actions, acted upon in the present, and shape the conditions encountered next.

## 3. The Present as a "Just-In-Time" Transition

If the past provides strict constraints, what happens in the present then? 

A computational metaphor provides a highly illustrative aid: reality operates somewhat like a "just-in-time" processing system. A system does not necessarily need to pre-render every future state in full detail before execution proceeds. The present can be understood as the active site of transition where inherited latent structures interact with current conditions. The future remains an unwritten trajectory until the specific processes that determine it—including the internal evaluations of living agents—are reached and resolved.

## 4. Recursive Participation and "Doing Otherwise"

Agency, then, is neither magical exemption from causality nor mechanical powerlessness; it is better to be understood as recursive participation. 

Consider a chess engine. A chess computer's move is entirely produced by the movement of electrons through hardware. Yet, it remains deeply meaningful to explain the move in terms of the engine's higher-level evaluation algorithms, rather than only in terms of underlying microphysics. Similarly, the fact that human cognitive processing has physical and historical causes does not make the deliberation irrelevant; that processing is the precise physical mechanism through which prior conditions are evaluated and carried forward. 

To summarize, human cognition (or free will) results from the emergence of the underlying physical, biochemical, socio-economic, historical systems set in place by the natural progression of time / entropy in the universe. To call this consciousness a gift granted to the subjective mind (by the deity of one's own choice) or attribute the fine-tuning of the universe itself that allows this cognition to exist in the first place to the anthropic principle remains entirely upto the reader's own subjective choosing. 

In any case, the radical reframing of the free agent as the byproduct of recursive participation lends oneself free from the classic demand of "could have done otherwise". Setting oneself aside from the ridid binary world of free wil god vs deterministic puppet does not require a person to accept a metaphysical alternative (that the ability to break the laws of physics would be possible if the exact state of the universe were rewound). It merely requires the acceptance of a conditional alternative: if different reasons, information, or internal models had been present, the system's (a person's) own organized processing would have yielded a different outcome.

## 5. Freedom Without Escape

The relentless debate between non-deterministic free will and strict determinism forces an unnecessary choice between being gods or puppets. By shifting the definition of agency, this binary can be abandoned.  

"True" "freedom" does not remain about escaping reality, stepping outside physical law, or pretending an agent owes nothing to the past. It is a developmental capacity. As an organized system learns, models its environment, identifies its constraints, and refines its internal processing, its capacity to navigate its field of possibilities grows.  

Human existence is constrained, relationally embedded, and bound to an inherited world. Yet organized systems process, respond, and alter what follows. Adopting the framework of constrained agency allows for an end to the demand for an escape from the structure of the universe, offering instead a way to take participation inside that structure seriously.
